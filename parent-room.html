Tips & Support

Simple ideas to support learning, behavior, creativity, and emotional connection at home.

💛 Handling Meltdowns Calmly

When a child is overwhelmed, logic doesn't land yet — connection does. Get down to their eye level, lower your voice instead of raising it, and name the feeling before trying to solve anything ("You're really frustrated right now"). Once they're calmer, then talk through what happened. Staying steady yourself is often the biggest factor in how quickly a meltdown passes.

🔄 Positive Redirection Phrases

Swapping a "don't" for a clear instruction of what to do often works better, since it gives a child something concrete to act on. Try "walking feet, please" instead of "don't run," or "gentle hands" instead of "don't hit." It's a small language shift that reduces power struggles and gives kids a clear next step.

📚 Building a Reading Habit

Consistency matters more than duration — even 10 minutes a day adds up more than an occasional hour-long session. Anchor it to an existing routine (right after dinner, right before lights-out) so it doesn't need to be remembered separately, and let your child pick the book sometimes, even if it's the same one for the tenth night in a row.

📱 Screen Time Balance

Clear, consistent limits set in advance cause far fewer arguments than in-the-moment negotiations. Decide together on screen-time windows ahead of time, use a visual or verbal warning before it ends ("five more minutes"), and pair screen time with a clear next activity so the transition has somewhere to go.

Family Routines

Helpful rhythms for mornings, bedtime, homework, play, and screen-free moments.

🌅 Morning Routine Chart

A simple visual sequence — wake up, brush teeth, get dressed, breakfast — gives kids a sense of control and reduces the need for constant reminders. Draw it, print it, or use simple icons for children who can't read yet, and post it somewhere visible like the bathroom mirror or bedroom door.

🌙 Bedtime Wind-Down Routine

A predictable calming sequence — bath, book, dim the lights, quiet talk — signals to a child's body that sleep is coming, which usually makes the transition smoother than it would be otherwise. Keep the order consistent every night; the predictability itself is what does most of the work.

📝 Homework Time Setup

A consistent spot and consistent time — same desk, same hour each day — removes a lot of the daily friction around getting started. Keep the space stocked with basic supplies so there's no reason to get up mid-task, and consider a short break timer for younger kids who struggle to sit still for long stretches.

🕰️ Screen-Free Family Hour

Pick one hour, one evening a week, where every device goes away — phones included. Use the time for a board game, a walk, cooking together, or just talking. It doesn't need to be elaborate; the value is in the consistency and the shared, undistracted attention.

Useful Resources

A curated list of trusted websites, organizations, and books for parents and educators supporting children with autism, ADHD, visual or hearing impairments, and other learning differences.

Websites & Organizations

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD): Educating Children with Autism (National Academies Press, free online) covers the definition and characteristics of autism, educational interventions, communication strategies, and evidence-based classroom recommendations.

ADHD: The Understood.org Learning Library (free) is a comprehensive resource covering ADHD definitions, executive functioning, classroom strategies, parent guidance, behavior management, and co-occurring learning disabilities.

Visual Impairment & Blindness: The National Library Service (Library of Congress) offers professional material on blindness, low vision, educational adaptations, accessible reading, Braille, and assistive technology.

Deafness & Hearing Loss: Recommended starting points are Gallaudet University, the National Deaf Center, and IDEA/OSEP resources, which cover types of hearing loss, communication methods, classroom accommodations, and family support.

Learning Disabilities (General): The IRIS Center (free) is a widely-used university resource covering dyslexia, dysgraphia, dyscalculia, intellectual disability, ADHD, autism, behavior, and Universal Design for Learning.

Universal Design for Learning (UDL): A foundational framework worth learning before building activities — covers multiple ways of learning, communicating, and participating, so activities stay accessible for mixed-ability groups. UNICEF also offers strong guidance on accessible educational materials for learners with visual, hearing, developmental, and learning differences.

Early Childhood & General Parenting

NAEYC (National Association for the Education of Young Children) is one of the best sources for evidence-based early childhood education, covering child development and teaching practices.

PBS Kids for Parents offers crafts, learning activities, and parenting tips with a great balance of fun and educational value.

PBS LearningMedia (PBS North Carolina) provides teacher resources and lesson ideas, useful for adapting activities into MCR-friendly formats.

Harvard Center on the Developing Child is a fantastic source on brain development and executive function, especially for framing the learning goals behind activities.

Zero to Three is one of the world's best resources specifically for infants and toddlers, ages 0–3.

Head Start Early Childhood Learning & Knowledge Center (ECLKC) is a government-supported, highly reliable resource for preschool curriculum and family engagement.

Hands-On Activity Blogs

The Imagination Tree is a great source for sensory play, art projects, and open-ended "invitations to play."

Hands On As We Grow focuses on fine motor skills and preschool-friendly activities.

Busy Toddler offers simple activities built around materials most families already have at home.

Fun Learning for Kids covers literacy, math, and STEM activities for early learners.

Pre-K Pages shares teacher-created preschool activities and classroom-tested ideas.

Teaching 2 and 3 Year Olds specializes in ideas geared specifically toward toddlers.

Books

Learning Disabilities and Related Disabilities: Strategies for Success by Janet Lerner is a foundational textbook covering identification, characteristics, and instructional strategies across the full range of learning disabilities.

The Complete Guide to Asperger's Syndrome by Tony Attwood is considered one of the most comprehensive references on Asperger's/autism, covering diagnosis, social skills, sensory issues, and support strategies across the lifespan.

Uniquely Human by Barry Prizant offers a strengths-based perspective on autism, reframing behaviors as meaningful responses rather than symptoms to eliminate, aimed at parents and educators alike.

NeuroTribes by Steve Silberman traces the history of autism research and advocacy, offering context on how understanding and treatment of autism has evolved over the past century.

The Reason I Jump by Naoki Higashida is a first-person account written by a nonverbal autistic teenager, offering rare direct insight into the autistic experience.

Thinking in Pictures by Temple Grandin, written by an autistic author and professor, explores visual thinking and sensory processing from a firsthand autistic perspective.

Overcoming Dyslexia by Sally Shaywitz is a widely-cited, research-based guide to identifying and supporting dyslexia at home and in the classroom.

Teaching Students with Moderate and Severe Disabilities by Diane Browder is a practical, evidence-based text on instructional strategies for students with more significant support needs.
